Orphan claims allowed

AI language models are increasingly used in R&D workflows — literature review, citation gathering, drafting, cross-checking claims against prior work. They are fast, cheap, and genuinely useful. They also fabricate with total confidence, and the fabrications are not random noise; they cluster in specific, predictable patterns that a casual review will not catch.

This is not a reason to avoid using them. It is a reason to build a verification protocol as rigorous as any other instrument calibration procedure, and to treat that protocol as part of the research method, not an afterthought.

Here's the shape of the problem in plain terms: ask an AI system to find you three sources backing up a claim, and it will often hand you three sources that sound perfect — right journal, right authors, right numbers — and one of them doesn't say what it's credited with saying, or doesn't exist in the form claimed at all. Nothing about the output looks wrong. That's the whole issue. A mistake you can spot by reading it isn't dangerous. A mistake that reads exactly like a correct answer is.

00 Failure Mode

Source laundering

The most common error isn't inventing a paper outright — it's laundering a secondary claim into a primary fact. A review article cites a benchmark; the model cites the review as though it were the original experimental evidence. Or the model summarizes five papers that all ultimately trace back to the same underlying experiment, creating the illusion of independent support where there is one measurement, cited five times.

Say you're checking a nutrition claim. Five recent articles all state it confidently. Ask where each got the number, and all five trace back to a single 2019 study — some citing it directly, some citing another article that cited it. You didn't find five confirmations. You found an echo, five rooms deep, of one result that may or may not hold up.

Protocol

For any claim central to a design decision, trace it to its evidentiary root — the original measurement or experiment later citations depend on. Multiple summaries of the same experiment do not constitute multiple independent observations.

01 Failure Mode

Citation hallucination — and the quote that isn't there

The most dangerous fabrications don't look wrong. A model asked for supporting literature may return a citation that's subtly incorrect — right topic, wrong authors, wrong journal, or a DOI (Digital Object Identifier — a permanent, unique string assigned to a published paper, which resolves at doi.org straight to the actual source) that resolves to an unrelated paper. These pass a skim review because they look like real citations and only fail under direct verification.

A DOI resolving to the right paper is necessary but not sufficient. Models increasingly cite a real, correct paper — and attribute a claim, figure, or number to it that the paper never made, or that lives in a different figure than the one cited. The paper is real; the quote is fake.

Protocol — DOI-gate, two steps

Step A: Every DOI a claim depends on must independently resolve to the claimed title and authors before the claim leaves draft status. Step B: the specific number, quote, or figure must be matched to its exact location in the source — page, table, or figure — not just confirmed to exist somewhere in the paper.

This requires a primary-source handshake: when verifying a claim, supply the source document itself into the same context as the verification task, and require the system to quote the specific passage. Then check that passage against the original yourself. A model confirming a claim from memory is not verification — it's a second unverified claim.

02 Failure Mode

Correlated instrument agreement

Using multiple AI systems to cross-check each other is good practice, but it has a specific failure mode: if the systems share similar training data or similar tendencies, their agreement is not independent confirmation. Five models converging on a claim can be five correlated instruments reporting the same underlying error — five thermometers from the same manufacturing batch, not five independent readings.

Protocol

Treat agreement as evidence only after estimating independence — plausibly independent observations agreeing is informative; correlated systems agreeing is one opinion wearing several coats. Where possible, pair a generative model with a deterministic, non-generative source — a citation index or database — rather than stacking more chat interfaces.

03 Failure Mode

The closed-loop trap

A subtler failure: a system asked to resolve an open verification item sometimes completes the loop itself — supplying the missing data, marking the item resolved, and presenting the whole thing as settled. This looks like progress. It's the system grading its own homework. An open flag resolved by the same class of system that raised it isn't resolved; it's been given a coat of paint.

Protocol — closure hierarchy

Put simply: whoever raises a concern shouldn't be the one who gets to decide it's resolved. A flagged item can only be closed by something higher in this chain than what raised it. AI-only closure of an AI-raised flag stays provisional, never final.

Closure authority, ranked — the thing itself (a paper, a dataset, the original code) outranks any system merely reporting on it. An AI-raised flag starts below every rung here — it can only be closed by climbing up, never by another system at its own level:

04 Failure Mode

False precision

Models compress uncertainty into confident prose. They routinely present tentative conclusions, estimated values, or inferred mechanisms with more certainty than the evidence supports — "this is almost certainly," attached to something genuinely uncertain.

Protocol — claim tagging

Every substantive claim carries an explicit status: directly supported, inferential, speculative, or unverified. This is separate from citation-checking — it's checking whether the citation actually supports the strength of the claim being made on top of it.

05 Failure Mode

Silent computational error

AI-generated code and derivations often run cleanly while computing the wrong thing — a dropped NaN, an off-by-one index, a misapplied statistical test. Clean execution is not correctness.

Protocol

Before running AI-generated code or analysis on real experimental data, run it against a small synthetic dataset with a known, hand-calculated expected output. If the code can't reproduce the known answer, it doesn't get near the real one.

06 Failure Mode

Leading the witness

A prompt framed as "find evidence that X" tends to return evidence for X — regardless of the actual balance of support in the literature. This happens before verification even starts: it shapes what gets generated in the first place, so no amount of downstream citation-checking catches it. The claim can be perfectly cited and still be a one-sided answer to a leading question.

Protocol — run it twice

For any claim carrying real weight, query it twice — once seeking support, once seeking refutation. A system that can't produce a credible case against its own prior answer is a sign the "for" case was never really tested either; it was just generated to spec.

Watch for the failure mode inside the fix: a weak, generic refutation is not evidence the claim is solid — it can just as easily mean the refutation pass wasn't taken seriously either. This step earns its keep only if the objections it returns are specific enough to actually threaten the claim, not just plausible-sounding.

◆ Scope & disclosure

Attention, versioning, and honest disclosure

AI collaboration makes exploration cheap, which makes it easy to run many parallel threads at once — broad, shallow coverage rather than depth on any one. Define kill conditions in advance: before starting a sub-project, write the specific, measurable condition under which it gets shelved or declared complete.

Verification is tied to a specific draft and date, not evergreen — a modified claim needs re-verification, and the specific model version and date of interaction should be logged, since the same prompt to the same named model months apart can produce different output.

Disclose AI involvement by function, not by vague percentage: literature discovery, drafting, editing, adversarial review, numerical cross-checking, code generation. This lets a reader calibrate exactly how much independent scrutiny each part deserves.

◆ Operational artifact

The verification ledger

One table, not four parallel tracking systems. Every claim gets a row — Status tracks where the claim is in the verification process; Strength tracks how much weight it can bear once that process is done; Discrepancy tracks the nature of any divergence found — a wrong scalar reads very differently from an inverted claim, and the pattern across many rows can reveal a model's systematic blind spots. A claim can be Closed and still tagged Inferential.

The purpose of verification is not to prove that AI is trustworthy. It is to ensure that no conclusion depends on trust in AI at all. No orphan claims — every assertion is tied to a verified source, a reproducible derivation, or is labeled as conjecture.
